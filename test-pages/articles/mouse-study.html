Painkiller shows promise in early tests

A common painkiller killed cancer cells in laboratory tests, according to researchers who published their work this week. The drug, which is sold cheaply in pharmacies, was tested against samples of blood cancer taken from patients who had agreed to donate them.

In the experiments, 62% of the treated samples showed signs of cell death within 48 hours, compared with 9% of the untreated samples. The team reported a 4% rise in a marker linked to cell repair, and said the effect was strongest at the highest dose.

The work was carried out in mice and in cell cultures. The researchers said the findings are preliminary and that the drug has not yet been tested in people. A team at the university will now look at whether the effect holds in larger groups of animals before any clinical trial is planned.

Specialists not involved in the study said the results were interesting but far from proof that the painkiller could treat cancer in patients. They said the dose needed for the effect in mice would be hard to reach safely in people, and that the drug can cause stomach bleeding at high doses.

The charity that funds blood cancer research said that most early laboratory results do not translate into treatments, and that patients should not change their medicines on the strength of news reports. The university said it would publish full results after peer review.
